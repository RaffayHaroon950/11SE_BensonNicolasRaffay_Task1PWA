Are We in a Sequel Era? (And Is That Bad?)

Why sequels keep winning — and what original films can do about it.

Benson to the Future 2 has arrived, and with it comes a familiar question: are audiences still hungry for sequels, or are studios simply playing it safe? As the follow-up to the surprise hit Benson to the Future, the film had expectations to meet — and plenty of scepticism to overcome.

The original Benson to the Future worked because it balanced humour, heart, and a playful take on time-travel tropes. Rather than relying solely on spectacle, it connected with audiences through character-driven storytelling. The sequel, however, had a harder task: expanding the story without losing what made the first film special.

One of the most noticeable trends in Benson to the Future 2 is its confidence. The film leans into the world it established, deepening relationships and raising the stakes without simply repeating familiar beats. Instead of retelling the same story with bigger explosions, it explores the consequences of the first film’s choices — a move that separates strong sequels from forgettable ones.

When Sequels Work — and When They Don’t

Not all sequels succeed, but this one highlights why audiences still show up for them. Familiar characters provide emotional investment, while new ideas keep the story fresh. When handled well, sequels feel less like cash-grabs and more like natural extensions of a story audiences care about.

That said, the dominance of sequels like Benson to the Future 2 does raise concerns. Original films often struggle for attention in a market saturated with recognisable titles. The challenge for studios going forward will be ensuring that nostalgia doesn’t replace innovation entirely.

Ultimately, Benson to the Future 2 proves that sequels aren’t inherently bad — but they do need purpose. When filmmakers respect their audience and their story, a sequel can feel just as exciting as the original.
